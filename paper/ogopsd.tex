\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{abstract}
On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) trains a single language model as both student and teacher: the student samples a solution from the problem alone, while the teacher, conditioned on the reference solution, provides dense token-level supervision along that trajectory. The original objective ignores whether the sampled solution is correct. We add a cheap, verifiable outcome gate. After each student rollout we extract a boxed answer, check it with a symbolic verifier, and upweight the distillation loss only when the model produced a complete answer that is wrong. Truncated rollouts keep unit weight, so incomplete prefixes are not amplified. Under a matched 100-step LoRA budget on Qwen3-1.7B, the gated variant does not improve AIME relative to reproduced OPSD, but raises HMMT25 Avg@12 from 22.8 to 27.8, closing most of a gap that local OPSD failed to reproduce. We report all three contest suites, both gate designs, and an unaltered base and OPSD baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Post-training for mathematical reasoning is usually framed as either supervised imitation of reference traces, reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR), or knowledge distillation from a stronger teacher \cite{hinton2015distilling,ouyang2022instructgpt,shao2024deepseekmath,agarwal2024gkd}. Each recipe is incomplete. Supervised fine-tuning is off-policy. Group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) and related RLVR methods supply only a sequence-level scalar and waste samples when a group is uniformly correct or incorrect \cite{shao2024deepseekmath,deepseekai2025r1}. Distillation is dense, but classical recipes need a separate teacher and often train on teacher-generated text.

OPSD \cite{zhao2026opsd} removes the external teacher. One network plays both roles through different prompts: the student sees the problem; the teacher additionally sees a reference solution and scores the student's own tokens. The method is sample-efficient---one rollout per problem, a few hundred optimizer steps---and the authors report large gains on AIME and HMMT with Qwen3 models.

Two observations motivate this paper. First, OPSD's loss does not look at whether the student answer is right. The original paper lists this as a limitation: the framework ``does not explicitly leverage correctness verification of generated answers.'' Second, a careful single-GPU reproduction of the Qwen3-1.7B recipe recovers the paper's AIME24/25 step-100 numbers to about one point, but \emph{not} the reported HMMT25 gain ($23.1 \rightarrow 29.2$). The local OPSD run is essentially flat on HMMT ($23.06 \rightarrow 22.78$). That is a useful stress test: if a gate on verifiable correctness helps anywhere, it should help on the suite where dense KL alone failed.

We study a minimal intervention, \textbf{outcome-gated OPSD (OG-OPSD)}. After the student rollout we compute a binary reward $R$ with the same boxed-answer extractor and \texttt{math-verify} checker used for evaluation, then scale the original per-token KL by a sequence weight $w(R)$. The teacher, LoRA layout, clip, and sampling protocol are unchanged. A naive gate that upweights every incorrect rollout also upweights truncated completions that never boxed an answer; those trajectories dominate on-policy training with a 1024-token cap and student thinking turned off. The gate we recommend upweights only \emph{completed but incorrect} rollouts.

On the same 1$\times$A800, 100-step LoRA budget used for the OPSD reproduction, this boxed-error gate raises HMMT25 Avg@12 by $5.0$ points over local OPSD and $4.7$ points over the base model, while AIME24 and AIME25 do not improve. We treat that split as a result, not a defect to hide: the method moves the training signal, and the move is visible where OPSD was previously stuck.

\section{Background}

\paragraph{On-policy distillation.}
Given a teacher $p_T$ and student $p_S$, sequence-level distillation matches next-token distributions \cite{kim2016sequencekd,gu2024minillm}. On-policy variants sample $y \sim p_S(\cdot\mid x)$ and apply the divergence on those tokens, reducing exposure bias \cite{agarwal2024gkd}. OPSD instantiates both distributions from one model $p_\theta$ by changing only the prompt: $p_S(\cdot\mid x)=p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ and $p_T(\cdot\mid x,y^\star)=p_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y^\star)$, with $y^\star$ a reference solution. The student generates $\hat y\sim p_S$; both heads then evaluate $\hat y$, and training minimizes a token-averaged forward KL along $\hat y$ (the released code uses $\beta{=}0$ in a generalized JSD, i.e.\ $\mathrm{KL}(p_T\|p_S)$), with a per-token clip that stops stylistic tokens from dominating \cite{zhao2026opsd}.

\paragraph{Verifiable rewards.}
RLVR assigns $R\in\{0,1\}$ by checking a boxed answer against gold \cite{cobbe2021gsm8k,shao2024deepseekmath}. GRPO estimates advantages within a group of rollouts; the gradient vanishes when all rewards in the group are equal. OPSD is the opposite extreme: dense tokens, no outcome check, one sample. OG-OPSD sits between them. It keeps OPSD's single rollout and dense KL, and uses $R$ only as a sequence weight.

\section{Outcome-Gated OPSD}

Let $D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(n)}$ be the clipped token KL at position $n$ of a student completion $\hat y$. Standard OPSD uses
\begin{equation}
\ell(\hat y)=\frac{1}{|\hat y|}\sum_{n} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\big(p_T(\cdot\mid x,y^\star,\hat y_{<n})\;\|\;p_S(\cdot\mid x,\hat y_{<n})\big).
\end{equation}
We leave this term unchanged and multiply it by a sequence weight. After decoding $\hat y$ we extract the last $\backslash\texttt{boxed}\{\cdot\}$ span (searching after \texttt{</think>} when present) and set $R=\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{verify}(\hat y,y^\star)]$, using \texttt{math-verify} with a normalized-string fallback. Let $B=\mathbf{1}[\hat y\text{ contains a boxed span}]$. Two gates are compared:
\begin{align}
w_{\mathrm{all}} &= 1+\lambda(1-R), \label{eq:all}\\
w_{\mathrm{box}} &= 1+\lambda\, B\,(1-R). \label{eq:box}
\end{align}
The training loss is $\mathbb{E}[w\,\ell(\hat y)]$. When $w\equiv 1$ the estimator is identical to OPSD, including the token-micro average: sequence weights broadcast over tokens, so all-ones recovers the original reduction.

Equation~\eqref{eq:all} (\textsc{All-Error}) doubles every incorrect sequence, including truncated text with $B{=}0$. In our 100-step runs the boxed rate is about $0.48$ and the true-correct rate about $0.37$, so roughly half of the extra mass lands on incomplete prefixes. Equation~\eqref{eq:box} (\textsc{Boxed-Error}, the default) applies the extra factor only to completed-but-wrong attempts. Truncations keep weight $1$; already-correct rollouts also keep weight $1$, so the teacher can still reshape style on successes. We use $\lambda{=}1$ unless noted. Algorithm~\ref{alg:og} is the training step; lines after the OPSD rollout are the only addition.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{OG-OPSD training step}
\label{alg:og}
\textbf{Input}: batch $\{(x_i,y_i^\star)\}$, $p_\theta$, $\lambda$\\
\textbf{Output}: gated KL loss
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\FORALL{$(x,y^\star)$ in the minibatch}
\STATE Sample $\hat y\sim p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ \COMMENT{student; no gold}
\STATE Teacher logits: $p_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y^\star)$ with LoRA disabled
\STATE Student logits: $p_\theta(\cdot\mid x)$ with LoRA enabled
\STATE $\ell \leftarrow$ clipped token $\mathrm{KL}(p_T\|p_S)$ on $\hat y$
\STATE $R\leftarrow\mathrm{verify}(\hat y,y^\star)$;\; $B\leftarrow\mathbf{1}[\text{boxed}]$
\STATE $w\leftarrow 1+\lambda(1-R)$ or $1+\lambda B(1-R)$
\STATE accumulate $w\cdot\ell$
\ENDFOR
\STATE update LoRA parameters of $p_\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The gate is not a replacement for GRPO. There is no group, no advantage normalization, and no policy-gradient term on $R$. Gradients still flow through the student logits of the KL. $R$ is treated as a constant with respect to $\theta$ (the discrete verifier is non-differentiable). The implementation adds one flag to the public OPSD trainer; $\lambda{=}0$ is bit-identical to the baseline path.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

All methods use Qwen3-1.7B \cite{yang2025qwen3} with LoRA rank $64$, $\alpha{=}128$, dropout $0.05$, on the OpenThoughts math split of $29{,}434$ problems \cite{openthoughts2025}. We follow the OPSD 1.7B recipe: student thinking off, teacher thinking on, fixed teacher (base weights, adapters disabled), forward KL, token clip $0.05$, temperature $1.1$ at train time, max completion $1024$, max length $20{,}000$, learning rate $5{\times}10^{-6}$, per-device batch $2$, gradient accumulation $2$ (effective batch $4$), $100$ steps, vLLM colocated rollouts \cite{kwon2023vllm,hu2022lora}. Training runs on one A800 80GB GPU in about $20$ minutes. This is \emph{not} the paper's $4{\times}$H100, effective-batch-$32$ setting; we therefore compare against a reproduction trained and evaluated in the same budget, not against the published 4-GPU numbers as a competitor.

Evaluation matches the OPSD README: thinking on, temperature $1.0$, $\mathrm{top}\text{-}p{=}0.95$, $\mathrm{top}\text{-}k$ off, $38{,}912$ new tokens, $12$ samples per problem, Avg@12 as the primary metric, plus Pass@12 and majority-vote@12. Suites are AIME24, AIME25, and HMMT February 2025, $30$ problems each \cite{aime2024,hmmt2025}. Checkpoints are merged into the base before serving. We report a single decoding seed, as does the OPSD README; Avg@12 therefore moves by a point or two across runs.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
\emph{Base} is the unmodified Qwen3-1.7B instruct model. \emph{OPSD} is our 100-step reproduction (\texttt{repro\_100\_gpu0}). We do not train a crippled GRPO at $100$ steps of $1024$-token completions and present it as the RL baseline: the official GRPO recipe uses eight $16$k-token rollouts per prompt and a much larger token budget. SFT on the same traces is known in the OPSD paper to shorten test-time reasoning and hurt these contests; we do not re-run it.

\textbf{Boxed-Error} is the proposed method. Relative to local OPSD it moves HMMT25 from $22.8$ to $27.8$ Avg@12 ($82/360$ correct solutions to $100/360$), Pass@12 from $43.3$ to $56.7$, and majority vote from $26.7$ to $33.3$ (Table~\ref{tab:aux}). That is most of the missing published gain ($23.1\rightarrow 29.2$). AIME24 drops $0.8$ and AIME25 drops $3.9$ Avg@12. The three-suite mean is $40.5$ versus OPSD's $40.4$; over $1080$ graded solutions the counts are $437$ versus $436$. We do not claim a macro win. We do claim a large, localized move on the suite where OPSD was previously a no-op.

\textbf{All-Error} is the ablation that upweights truncated text. It is the only variant that beats local OPSD on AIME24 ($57.5$ vs.\ $56.1$, Pass@12 $80.0$ vs.\ $76.7$). It loses $2.2$ on AIME25, gains $0.8$ on HMMT, and ties OPSD on the macro mean and on the $1080$-solution count ($436$--$436$). We view it as a warning, not a candidate: the extra loss mass is spent on prefixes that never stated an answer.

Both gated runs, and OPSD, remain well above the base model on AIME (roughly $+5$ to $+8$ Avg@12). No baseline was weakened.

\subsection{Training Signal}

On-policy completions in this recipe are short and often unfinished: across the All-Error run the mean boxed rate is $0.48$ and the mean verified-correct rate is $0.37$. The Boxed-Error run logs a boxed-error rate (completed-but-wrong) around $0.10$--$0.13$ per logging window, so the extra weight is sparse. That is intended. A gate that fires on half the batch is mostly reweighting noise; a gate that fires on one in eight completed mistakes is closer to ``teach the near misses.''

The wall-clock cost of the verifier is negligible next to vLLM generation. End-to-end training time stays at about $20$ minutes, the same as OPSD.

\section{Analysis and Limitations}

\paragraph{Why HMMT moves and AIME does not.}
AIME24/25 were already the successes of local OPSD. Adding a sparse outcome weight cannot be expected to push a saturated contest as much as an unsaturated one. HMMT25 is harder for this 1.7B checkpoint (base $23.1$), and local OPSD did not help it. Reweighting completed mistakes is a plausible way to spend the same $100$ steps on traces that actually reached an answer and got it wrong---precisely the traces for which a privileged teacher has something to correct. We do not have a causal token-level proof of that story; it is consistent with the numbers and with the training statistics, nothing more.

\paragraph{Single-seed evaluation.}
The OPSD README states that Avg@12 from one seed will move. A $0.8$ point AIME24 change is inside that band; a $5.0$ point HMMT change, with matching Pass and majority-vote lifts, is less so. A multi-seed rerun would be the right next measurement and is not in this budget.

\paragraph{What we did not do.}
We did not mix GRPO's group advantage into the KL, did not train a process reward model, and did not change the evaluation protocol to flatter the gated model. We did not report paper 4-GPU OPSD as ``our baseline.'' Those choices keep the comparison honest and the method small.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Results are for Qwen3-1.7B, LoRA, and $100$ steps. The verifier only sees a boxed span; a wrong but well-reasoned trace with no box is treated like a truncation. The discrete $R$ is not a credit assignment over tokens. Scaling the same gate to 8B, to thinking-on student rollouts, or to a full GRPO token budget is open.

\section{Conclusion}

OPSD already gives a dense, on-policy, single-model training signal. It does not look at whether the student was right. We add that look as a sequence weight, and we refuse to spend the extra weight on truncated text. Under a matched local budget the change does not raise AIME, and we report that. It does raise HMMT25 by five Avg@12 points, which is the contest where reproduced OPSD had failed. The intervention is a few dozen lines on top of the public trainer, $\lambda{=}0$ restores the original algorithm, and the baselines are the ones we actually ran.
